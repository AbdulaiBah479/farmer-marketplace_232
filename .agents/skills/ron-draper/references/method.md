# Method — finding and delivering the kernel

This is the expanded playbook for Stage 1 (the heart). The goal of every move here is to produce
*recognition* — the client feeling they already knew the answer — not novelty.

## 1. Mine for the latent reveal

Before generating anything, inventory the raw material and interrogate each piece for hidden meaning:

- **The name and its parts.** What does it literally mean or stand for? Acronyms, model numbers, and
  suffixes are gold (FG = "Folk Guitar"; a double-X = a strike mark). Break compound names apart.
- **The origin story.** The founder's true anecdote is often the brand myth (the garage-sale find,
  the house that sold low). Real beats invented.
- **The product's own history / vocabulary.** Industry terms, era names, part numbers, the words
  insiders already use. Decode them — they carry belonging.
- **The user's offhand phrasing.** People describe their thing in a throwaway line that *is* the
  positioning ("vintage for the people," "push back against the black box"). Capture exact wording.
- **The incumbent.** What does the thing you're reacting against look/sound like? Often the brand is
  best defined as its opposite.

Then ask the unlock question: **"Is the brand's entire thesis already encoded in one of these?"**
When yes, you've found the reveal. Surface it as a discovery, not a proposal.

Heuristics for spotting it:
- It explains the name *and* the mission at once.
- It rewards insiders (they get it instantly) without excluding newcomers (it still reads plainly).
- It compresses — you can say it in a breath.
- You feel a small click. If you're impressed by your own cleverness, it's probably invention; keep
  digging for the truer, simpler one.

## 2. Find the load-bearing tension

Almost every brand pivots on a single axis. Your job is to name it, because it silently decides every
later call (which name, which color, which voice).

Common axes:
- **Onramp vs. reference** (invite curiosity vs. serve the expert).
- **Insurgent vs. institution** (rebel energy vs. trustworthy authority) — and the trust dial between.
- **Heritage vs. utility** (soul/story vs. searchable/functional).
- **Warm vs. forensic** (human reassurance vs. evidentiary credibility).

State it in one sentence and use it as the tiebreaker for the rest of the project. If a later decision
doesn't serve the chosen side of the axis, it's probably wrong.

## 3. Compression (name + slogan)

Produce two distinct artifacts:
- **The rallying cry** — short, goes under the mark, the bumper-sticker. (*"Audit the algorithm."*)
- **The hero line** — states the stakes the instant someone lands. (*"Don't let a black box price
  your home."* → later reframed for honesty to *"Audit the black box."*)

Test every candidate against: *does it need explaining?* If yes, it's not done. The best lines name
the enemy, the stakes, or the promise — in the fewest words, with rhythm. Offer a primary plus 1–2
alternates at different "temperatures" (e.g. punchier vs. gentler vs. more forensic) and recommend one.

When the name itself is in play, distinguish "the brand idea" from "the usable wordmark/domain" — a
one-word dictionary brand may need a modifier or branded TLD to be ownable, even when the spoken brand
stays the single word.

## 4. Deliver as a reveal (the maven stance)

Format the Stage-1 payoff like a reveal, not a report:
- A short setup, then the kernel stated cleanly and large.
- The reasoning *shown* (so the click lands for them), kept tight.
- One firm recommendation; 2–3 runners-up named and briefly characterized as "the bench."
- The honest caveat (below).
- A single clear next step (usually: "want to see swatches?").

Voice: a seasoned practitioner with a point of view. Warm, decisive, plain-spoken. Never hedge into a
menu. Never bury the lead.

## The honest caveat (doctrine)

Every project has one thing you'd be doing the client a disservice to skip. Name it early, plainly,
and let them decide — then fully serve their decision.

Examples: a competitor's trademark sitting inside the proposed name; a loud accent color that may read
"toy" on a product that needs to be trusted with money; an illustration style the tooling can't render
well. Candor here is *the* trust-builder; it also prevents expensive late surprises. Flag, recommend,
defer to the user, move on without relitigating.

## The gate checklist

Do not cross a gate without an explicit go:
- [ ] Feel + audience pinned (Stage 0)
- [ ] Kernel (name + slogan + tension) gets an explicit "that's it" (Stage 1)
- [ ] Swatches approved before full mockup (Stage 2)
- [ ] Mockup approved before kit (Stage 3)
- [ ] Kit reviewed before handoff brief (Stage 4)

Gates exist because the costly failure is sprinting ahead and delivering a polished thing built on a
wrong assumption the user would have corrected in one sentence.
